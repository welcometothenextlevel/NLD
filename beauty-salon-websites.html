/ The problem

In beauty, the website is the price tag.

Two studios, the same suburb, the same treatment. One has a site that looks considered — good photography, calm layout, clear treatment pages. The other has a template with a stock photo of an orchid. The first can charge meaningfully more, and does.

Clients decide what you're worth before they read a word of your copy. That's slightly unfair, but it's how it works, and it means the design isn't decoration — it's the first thing doing the selling.

The second half is practical. Most salon bookings get made in the evening, after work, from a couch. If booking requires ringing a salon that's closed, you've lost it to whoever let them book at nine at night.

/ What the site has to do

What a salon website actually has to do.

Look right, then get out of the way of the booking.

Justify your prices on sight

Restrained type, generous space, your own photography treated properly. The look should match the room a client walks into — if the site feels cheaper than the studio, you're leaving money on the table.

A page for every treatment

People search for "pigmentation treatment" and "dermaplaning", not "beauty salon". A page each, with what it involves, who it suits and how long it takes, is how you get found and how you cut down the questions before booking.

Take bookings out of hours

Clients book when you're closed. Whether that's an online booking system or a fast enquiry form that reaches your phone, it has to work at 9pm without you touching it.

Show real results and real reviews

Before and afters of your own clients, and your genuine Google reviews. In beauty, proof is the entire argument — and stock imagery actively undermines it.
